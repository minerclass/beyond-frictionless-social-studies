Abstract

This article examines the paradox of frictionless learning in social studies classrooms shaped by generative artificial intelligence (AI). Although AI-driven tools promise individualized feedback and efficiency, they also risk undermining the deliberate cognitive and social struggles essential to deeper understanding and democratic engagement. Drawing on constructivist theories (Dewey, Vygotsky, Piaget, and Freire) and media critiques (McLuhan, Carr), we argue that friction—or productive intellectual disequilibrium—drives conceptual growth and ethical deliberation, processes core to social studies’ civic mission.

We introduce two complementary frameworks—C.O.R.E. (Critical Thinking, Openness, Respect, Engagement) and H.E.A.R.T. (Honesty, Empathy, Accountability, Responsibility, Thoughtfulness)—to help educators integrate AI in ways that sustain, rather than supplant, rigorous inquiry, collaborative discourse, and moral reflection. Through concrete examples, we illustrate how friction can be intentionally reintroduced in AI-mediated lessons to combat misinformation, highlight algorithmic bias, and bolster equitable participation. We further discuss policy, professional development, and research pathways that empower teachers to maintain technology as a scaffold for critical thinking rather than a substitute for learner-driven exploration. In doing so, we demonstrate a balanced, ethically grounded approach to AI adoption that preserves social studies’ commitment to informed, engaged, and equitable citizenship.

I. Introduction

The accelerating integration of generative artificial intelligence (AI) within educational environments presents profound implications for teaching and learning, particularly within the context of social studies education. AI-powered tools such as ChatGPT, adaptive learning platforms, and other automated pedagogical technologies promise unprecedented access, customization, and efficiency. These tools offer immediate responses, individualized scaffolding, and frictionless experiences that seem to optimize learning pathways, particularly for students navigating complex informational landscapes. However, beneath the apparent benefits of technological innovation lies a fundamental paradox deeply embedded within the philosophical, cognitive, and ethical frameworks of education itself.

This paradox is rooted in the longstanding educational theories articulated by John Dewey (1938), Lev Vygotsky (1978), and Paulo Freire (1970), who collectively emphasize the importance of productive struggle, critical reflection, social interaction, and experiential engagement as central pillars of genuine learning and intellectual development.

This tension between frictionless technological affordances and the necessary intellectual friction integral to deep learning is more than theoretical—it raises critical questions about the nature, quality, and equity of learning experiences mediated by generative AI. The pedagogical concerns presented by the frictionless experience of AI tools reflect Nicholas Carr’s extensive analysis in The Glass Cage (2014) and his recent Superbloom (2025), both of which critique the pervasive cultural enthusiasm for automation and seamless user experiences. Carr argues persuasively that automation, including educational automation, can diminish essential cognitive skills and capacities for critical reflection, ultimately reducing human resilience and agency.

Within educational contexts, Carr’s concerns manifest clearly in the ways AI may inadvertently discourage students from engaging deeply with challenging content, bypassing opportunities for intellectual struggle that foster genuine understanding and cognitive growth.

Complementing this perspective is the influential work of Marshall McLuhan (1964), who famously argued that the medium through which information is conveyed shapes cognition and understanding more powerfully than the content itself. The contemporary application of McLuhan’s insights becomes particularly salient in the context of AI-driven education, where students increasingly rely on platforms that deliver simplified summaries, instant answers, and automatically curated knowledge. This shift away from the critical examination of primary sources toward algorithmically mediated interpretations risks eroding students' capacities to critically assess and construct knowledge independently, competencies particularly vital to civic participation and historical inquiry.

Further amplifying these concerns, research by Sherry Turkle (2011) highlights the social dimensions of educational experiences, noting that meaningful learning often occurs within rich contexts of human interaction and dialogue. Lev Vygotsky’s (1978) foundational concept of the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) similarly underscores the essential role of guided social interaction and peer scaffolding in the development of higher-order cognitive skills. The advent of AI technologies threatens this essential social component of learning by prioritizing individualized and automated interactions, thus reducing opportunities for the collaborative intellectual struggle inherent in Vygotsky’s conceptualization of effective learning environments.

Drawing upon these rich theoretical foundations, this article employs the constructivist theories of Dewey, Vygotsky, Freire, and Piaget as lenses through which to critically examine the implications of generative AI in educational settings. Specifically, it explores how frictionless technological tools, despite their considerable appeal and practical convenience, risk exacerbating existing inequalities, reinforcing algorithmic biases, facilitating the spread of misinformation, and undermining fundamental educational values of critical thinking, ethical reasoning, and democratic engagement.

To address these multifaceted challenges, this article proposes and elaborates upon two innovative frameworks, C.O.R.E. (Critical Thinking, Openness, Respect, Engagement) and H.E.A.R.T. (Honesty, Empathy, Accountability, Responsibility, Thoughtfulness), designed explicitly to integrate technoskeptical perspectives with critical digital citizenship practices. These frameworks provide structured pedagogical guidance for educators, enabling them to intentionally navigate the integration of AI technologies in ways that enhance, rather than diminish, students’ capacities for productive struggle, critical reflection, and social interaction.

The central objective of this article is therefore twofold: first, to critically interrogate how the current deployment of generative AI in classrooms may inadvertently erode essential educational practices and outcomes; and second, to present practical, theory-driven strategies for educators to reclaim productive friction in learning environments increasingly dominated by automated technologies. In doing so, the discussion aligns closely with the National Council for the Social Studies’ (NCSS) vision for education that emphasizes civic competence, critical inquiry, and democratic participation (NCSS, 2013). Ultimately, this work seeks to articulate a clear pathway forward, advocating for an ethically informed, critically engaged, and pedagogically sound integration of AI technologies into social studies education that upholds and enhances the fundamental goals of democratic education.

II. Theoretical Foundations: Constructivism, Media Theory, and Productive Struggle

A deeper understanding of the complexities surrounding generative AI in social studies education rests upon several overlapping theoretical lineages. Constructivist thinkers like John Dewey, Lev Vygotsky, Jean Piaget, and Paulo Freire stress that authentic learning emerges from the dynamic interplay between learners and their social and cognitive contexts, rather than from passively receiving prepackaged information (Dewey, 1938; Vygotsky, 1978; Piaget, 1970; Freire, 1970). This emphasis on struggle, interaction, and experiential engagement lays a powerful foundation for analyzing how frictionless AI platforms can inadvertently undermine the very mechanisms that promote inquiry, higher-order thinking, and civic preparation.

2.1 Dewey’s Experiential Learning and Democratic Education

A central component of Dewey’s (1938) vision is that education should be firmly grounded in experience, with learning experiences designed to be both ‘engaging’ and ‘purposeful.’ Students, Dewey argued, must actively participate in constructing knowledge rather than absorbing it passively. This ideal positions the learner as an agent—someone who experiments, questions, and reflects—and envisions teachers as facilitators rather than mere transmitters of predetermined facts. Dewey also linked experiential learning explicitly to democratic values, maintaining that a robust public sphere relies on citizens capable of critical reflection, dialogue, and collaborative problem-solving.

Generative AI systems, while offering tailored resources and on-demand answers, can potentially flatten Dewey’s expansive notion of experience into a series of quick, frictionless interactions. Students bypass the messy, iterative processes that nurture self-discovery: grappling with original documents, trial and error, and the raw sense of uncertainty or disequilibrium that typically propels inquiry. Rather than immersing themselves in a process of exploration, they can seamlessly jump to an ‘answer’ delivered via AI suggestions or chatbots. This bypassing of the intellectual labor, which Dewey held to be foundational to genuine learning, raises troubling implications for the development of civic competence and critical consciousness—vital outcomes in social studies education.

2.2 Vygotsky’s Sociocultural Theory and the Zone of Proximal Development

Where Dewey highlights experiential learning in direct interaction with one’s environment, Vygotsky (1978) foregrounds social interaction as the linchpin of cognitive development. His concept of the Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) articulates a space where learners benefit from appropriate scaffolding—provided by peers, teachers, or other knowledgeable agents—to stretch beyond their current mastery. In this zone, learners strive, but do not flounder. Productive struggle occurs under guided mediation, ensuring learners remain actively engaged in problem-solving and internalizing new cognitive structures.

While AI could theoretically be leveraged to scaffold learning experiences in alignment with Vygotskian principles, frictionless platforms may paradoxically shift the locus of control from a communal, interactive process to an individualized, automated one. Instead of wrestling collaboratively with multifaceted historical events, for example, students may rely on AI systems to reduce interpretive complexity, thereby diminishing the social dialogues that Vygotsky argued are pivotal for meaning-making. The risk is a reductive transformation of the ZPD—from a shared space of lively debate, perspective-taking, and reciprocal feedback—into an algorithmically optimized track that prescribes knowledge with minimal relational engagement. In losing the collaborative dimension, the learning experience becomes isolated and potentially decontextualized, inhibiting the development of empathy, cultural responsiveness, and collective problem-solving—key societal goals of social studies.

2.3 Piaget’s Stages of Cognitive Development: Disequilibrium and Assimilation

Complementing Dewey’s experiential emphasis and Vygotsky’s social lens, Piaget’s (1970) cognitive-developmental framework highlights the role of disequilibrium—moments when students confront information or experiences that do not neatly align with their existing schemas. Piaget contended that learning flourishes in these transformative junctures, prompting either assimilation of novel information into existing cognitive frameworks or accommodation that reconstructs one’s schemas entirely. The creative tensions in these processes propel deeper understanding and conceptual growth.

Although generative AI can serve as a rich knowledge repository, frictionless solutions frequently offer illusions of clarity devoid of the essential disequilibrium. For instance, a student exploring the causes of a historical conflict might, through a single AI query, receive an authoritative-sounding summary, bypassing the struggle of reconciling conflicting sources or puzzling through biases and gaps. Consequently, learners may miss out on precisely those moments of cognitive tension that Piaget identifies as catalysts for profound conceptual change. Moreover, if AI fails to recognize a learner’s readiness level or the nuance of their prior knowledge, it may supply content too simplified to spark disequilibrium, impeding deeper inquiry or critical thinking.

2.4 Freire’s Critique of the “Banking Model”

Underscoring these cognitive and social perspectives is Paulo Freire’s (1970) philosophical stance against what he terms the “banking model” of education—where teachers deposit information into passive recipients. While Dewey, Vygotsky, and Piaget emphasize different dimensions of active, experiential learning, Freire sharpens the critique by foregrounding issues of power, liberation, and human agency. For Freire, authentic learning is inherently political, requiring students to question, resist, and engage collectively rather than simply consume curated knowledge. Education that eliminates or reduces the necessary friction of inquiry and self-directed exploration can result in learned helplessness or compliance, hampering the development of critical consciousness essential for democratic transformation.

In AI-mediated classrooms, frictionless functionality can inadvertently replicate this banking model. Automated lesson delivery, quick feedback loops, and tidy “answers” can sideline the dialogical processes fundamental to Freire’s vision of emancipation. Students could become dependent on AI to resolve uncertainties, ceding their roles as co-creators of meaning. Rather than fostering a sense of agency, where learners grapple with real-world problems and injustices, frictionless interfaces risk promoting an uncritical acquiescence to machine outputs—what Freire would label a dehumanizing transaction rather than an empowering educational encounter.

2.5 Media Theory and Technology Critiques: McLuhan, Carr, and Technoskepticism

While constructivist theorists speak compellingly to the necessity of intellectual and social friction, media theorists such as Marshall McLuhan (1964) and Nicholas Carr (2010; 2025) extend these critiques to examine the influence of modern technologies on cognition, social relations, and public discourse. McLuhan’s notion that “the medium is the message” directs attention to how the forms of technological mediation shape not only what we learn, but how we think. Carr’s work, including both The Shallows (2010) and Superbloom (2025), underscores how frictionless digital experiences fragment attention, reduce reflective processes, and accelerate shallow engagement with information.

Technoskeptical approaches in social studies, exemplified by Krutka, Metzger, and Seitz (2022) and Heath, Krutka, Jarke, and Macgilchrist (2024), foreground the sociopolitical ramifications of adopting new media in educational contexts. These researchers urge educators to question the broader trade-offs posed by frictionless AI solutions, stressing how algorithmic biases can amplify existing inequities and how convenience can erode the democratic value of critical, collective engagement. By examining AI through the combined lenses of constructivist theorists and media scholars, we gain a richer, more urgent perspective on how frictionlessness can undermine the cultivation of autonomy, reflection, and civic agency—the explicit aims of democratic, transformative social studies education.

2.6 Productive Struggle and Democratic Citizenship

Together, Dewey’s experiential learning, Vygotsky’s sociocultural focus, Piaget’s structural cognitive development, and Freire’s insistence on liberation and agency provide a coherent blueprint for understanding why productive struggle is indispensable. When learners must wrestle with content, each step of that wrestle fosters adaptive thinking, deeper conceptual grasp, ethical considerations, and participatory competencies. Social studies, committed to developing informed citizens who can interpret history, evaluate sources, and navigate civic challenges, relies profoundly on these friction-laden processes.

Media theory further heightens the stakes, warning that frictionless platforms often undermine these processes by isolating learners, masking biases, and privileging consumption over creation. The net effect is a diminishment of the intellectual, moral, and civic capacities that robust social studies curricula strive to nurture. This second section thus establishes the theoretical underpinnings of our argument, illustrating how frictionless AI learning may undermine essential constructivist tenets, hamper the collaborative nature of inquiry, and replicate the “banking model” that Freire deplored. It also sets the stage for a systematic exploration of how educators can intentionally design instructional environments that reconcile AI’s efficiency with the human-driven struggles vital to democratic learning.

III. The Paradoxes of Frictionless AI: Misinformation, Algorithmic Bias, and Eroded Agency

Having established the theoretical underpinnings of why productive struggle is indispensable for holistic, democratic education, it is crucial to examine how frictionless AI tools can unintentionally undermine that struggle in practice. Social studies educators—and indeed all educators—are grappling with a new wave of algorithm-driven resources that, while promising efficiency and personalized support, also risk intensifying several longstanding challenges in education: the spread of misinformation, the entrenchment of social inequities through algorithmic bias, and the erosion of human agency.

3.1 Misinformation and Disinformation Amplified

Digital misinformation and disinformation have long been a concern for social studies education, given the discipline’s focus on civic competence and historical analysis (NCSS, 2013; Kahne & Rogers, 2024). The rise of generative AI has exacerbated these problems, as AI-driven tools can convincingly synthesize dubious or outright false information at speeds and scales previously unimaginable (DiResta, 2024; Simon, Altay, & Mercier, 2023). A frictionless user experience—e.g., receiving instantaneous summaries or auto-generated explanations—may leave little room for critical interrogation of sources.

Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018) famously documented how falsehoods can spread up to six times faster than truths on social media platforms, a phenomenon likely to intensify when AI systems prioritize engaging content over verified accuracy. In a social studies classroom, for instance, a student could query an AI chatbot about a contentious historical event and receive an authoritative-sounding yet factually distorted response. Without the friction of evaluating multiple sources, conflicting viewpoints, or engaging in teacher-guided inquiry, learners risk absorbing misinformation unchecked. This not only undermines their capacity for critical thinking—central to social studies—but also imperils democratic health, as misinformed citizens find themselves increasingly susceptible to ideological manipulation.

3.2 Algorithmic Bias and the Deepening of Inequities

Equally concerning is the capacity of frictionless AI to inadvertently reinforce socio-cultural biases. As Joy Buolamwini (2023) demonstrates in her work on facial recognition, AI systems often reflect the biases inherent in their training data, systematically disadvantaging marginalized communities. Within an educational framework, algorithmic personalization can similarly manifest subtle forms of ‘technological redlining,’ whereby certain student demographics receive less challenging or more remedial content, thus restricting their opportunities for rigorous intellectual engagement.

If a student’s performance metrics—on AI-driven platforms—label them as ‘struggling,’ the system might reduce reading complexity or offer truncated summaries, effectively diminishing the cognitive friction Piaget (1970) deemed necessary to spur deep learning. Further, generative AI might fail to account for cultural diversity in historical narratives, inadvertently perpetuating a monolithic or Eurocentric worldview (Lee, Moore, & Hancock, 2023). The net result is a cycle of academic tracking where certain students engage in substantive, dialectical learning while others are channeled into oversimplified digital experiences. This unintentional digital segregation is especially dangerous in social studies, a field committed to inclusivity, equitable civic participation, and the exploration of multiple historical perspectives (Berson & Berson, 2023).

3.3 Eroded Agency and Passive Learning

Paulo Freire’s (1970) critique of the “banking model” of education resonates powerfully in an AI-mediated landscape, where frictionless solutions simplify tasks to the point that learners become passive consumers rather than active co-creators of knowledge. Students dependent on AI to resolve ambiguities or generate essays may quickly come to view knowledge as a static commodity delivered via seamless digital pipelines. As Vygotsky’s (1978) sociocultural theory suggests, authentic cognitive development unfolds in the collaborative negotiation of meaning and within social contexts—none of which can be replaced by a purely algorithmic interface.

This diminished sense of agency is not solely an individual matter; it also carries dire implications for civic life and democratic engagement. Dewey (1938) underscored that a functioning democracy depends on citizens who can think critically, confront complexity, and engage in sustained inquiry. When frictionless AI tools short-circuit the very processes—like reconciling contradictory accounts, parsing original sources, or collaboratively debating contentious issues—that cultivate these dispositions, we risk normalizing an unreflective, compliant citizenry. This dynamic positions AI not as a vehicle for empowerment, but rather as a quiet force of de-skilling, circumventing the moral and intellectual growth central to social studies pedagogy.

3.4 The New “Shallows”: Technological Convenience Over Cognitive Depth

Nicholas Carr’s (2010) warning in The Shallows that digital environments encourage rapid scanning instead of deliberate engagement is magnified in the era of generative AI. While earlier digital tools still required some measure of searching, reading, and selecting among sources, AI chatbots and recommender systems often package information into neat, authoritative-sounding answers. This phenomenon resonates with McLuhan’s (1964) claim that the medium itself dictates how individuals process content; frictionless interactions further amplify this effect, channeling students toward an acceptance of secondhand narratives and sanitized conclusions.

Ethan Mollick (2024) and the Stanford AI Lab (Wang & Srivastava, 2025) have both noted that while AI can indeed streamline certain academic processes, this convenience might undermine students’ willingness or ability to dive into ambiguous, open-ended explorations. By relegating them to reactive consumers of pre-interpreted knowledge, frictionless AI effectively erodes the kind of ‘desirable difficulties’ that lead to robust, long-term learning (Bjork & Bjork, 2011). The outcome is a generation of learners adept at handling structured tasks but underprepared for grappling with real-world complexities—precisely the terrain where social studies seeks to foster citizenship, critical inquiry, and ethical discernment.

3.5 Implications for Social Studies Educators

Educators, therefore, confront a critical fork in the road. The frictionless promise of AI—instant feedback, expedited research, algorithmically curated historical events—may seem like an instructional boon, particularly under time constraints and standards demands. Yet the risks of misinformation, bias reinforcement, and diminished agency loom large. In social studies, more than in many other disciplines, the call to foster informed democratic citizens necessitates an intentional commitment to constructive struggle and reflective questioning.

This section has outlined how generative AI, left to its own devices, can exacerbate entrenched inequities, propagate misinformation, and undermine the very intellectual habits that define a healthy civic life. In doing so, it underscores the urgency for educators to critically interrogate AI integration and design learning experiences that remain faithful to the sociocultural, experiential, and emancipatory principles central to constructivist and critical pedagogies. Section IV will propose a structured framework—built upon the C.O.R.E. and H.E.A.R.T. models—for harnessing AI’s advantages without sacrificing the human, democratic, and ethical dimensions of social studies education.

IV. Harnessing AI with C.O.R.E. and H.E.A.R.T.: Reintroducing Productive Struggle

Having charted how frictionless AI tools risk undermining essential learning processes and democratic imperatives, the question naturally arises: how might educators embrace the affordances of AI without sacrificing the deliberate struggle, critical inquiry, and civic formation essential to social studies? In this section, we propose two complementary frameworks—C.O.R.E. (Critical Thinking, Openness, Respect, Engagement) and H.E.A.R.T. (Honesty, Empathy, Accountability, Responsibility, Thoughtfulness)—that integrate a technoskeptical lens with constructivist, experiential pedagogy. These frameworks are designed to guide educators in structuring classroom practices where AI can serve as a catalyst for deep learning rather than a shortcut that bypasses intellectual struggle.

4.1 From Technoskepticism to Transformative Practice

The C.O.R.E. and H.E.A.R.T. frameworks emerged from the recognition that social studies educators, in particular, confront a dual imperative: on one hand, to prepare students for a digital age saturated with algorithmic media and AI-driven tools; on the other, to uphold the discipline’s dedication to reflective inquiry, civic competence, and social justice (NCSS, 2013). Building on work by Krutka, Metzger, and Seitz (2022), Heath et al. (2024), and Berson & Berson (2023), who all call for technoskepticism in education, these models operationalize the critical dispositions that educators and students need to sustain meaningful, ethically grounded learning in AI-mediated contexts.

Technoskepticism: This does not imply a rejection of technology but, rather, a commitment to questioning who benefits from a particular tool, what biases and hidden costs might exist, and how adopting it might transform social and cognitive processes. Technoskepticism invites educators to slow down, look for unintended consequences, and frame AI as an object of inquiry rather than a neutral resource.

Within this broader stance, the C.O.R.E. and H.E.A.R.T. frameworks provide actionable pathways for reintroducing friction and ensuring that AI remains a scaffold rather than a substitute for learner-driven exploration.

4.2 The C.O.R.E. Framework: Cognitive and Civic Anchors

  • Critical Thinking: At the center of C.O.R.E. is the imperative to analyze, evaluate, and interrogate both content and context. Rather than accepting AI-generated outputs at face value, students engage in source triangulation, comparative analysis, and reasoned debate, mirroring Piaget’s emphasis on disequilibrium and Vygotsky’s concept of socially mediated learning.
  • Openness: Social studies thrives on encountering multiple perspectives—historical, cultural, ideological. Openness within the framework fosters an environment where AI-supplied information serves as a starting point rather than a final word. Whether examining competing narratives of a historical event or diverse interpretations of current policy, students and teachers practice open-minded inquiry by layering AI responses with human perspectives and domain expertise.
  • Respect: A frictionless environment can undermine the communal ethos of learning. Respect underscores the importance of empathy, civility, and equitable participation, especially when AI-facilitated tasks risk isolating learners. Classroom dialogues, peer reviews, and structured debates ensure that all voices—including those marginalized by algorithmic biases—are recognized and valued.
  • Engagement: Moving beyond passive consumption requires hands-on, problem-based activities. Engagement prescribes intentional collaboration, guided inquiry, and project-based tasks that amplify real-world relevance. For instance, students might design a research project that systematically evaluates AI-generated historical summaries, cross-referencing them with primary sources to expose errors, omissions, or biases.

Collectively, C.O.R.E. provides a cognitive-civic anchor: by intertwining critical and civil capacities, educators guide students toward deeper reflection, sustained intellectual rigor, and participatory learning.

4.3 The H.E.A.R.T. Framework: Ethical and Emotional Dimensions

  • Honesty: Echoing Freire’s rejection of dehumanizing ‘banking’ models, the principle of honesty compels learners and educators to confront AI outputs transparently. Students learn to question the authenticity of AI responses, acknowledge uncertainties, and identify potential biases or errors. In social studies, this might translate into students verifying AI-generated versions of historical events against multiple archival documents.
  • Empathy: Vygotsky’s sociocultural framework underscores how knowledge is co-constructed. Empathy expands that social dimension by ensuring AI-driven tasks do not occlude genuine human interaction. Educators can, for example, incorporate collaborative reflection sessions where students share their emotional and intellectual responses to AI-supplied content—thus ensuring that human connectivity and understanding remain central.
  • Accountability: Accountability addresses the moral weight of adopting AI tools. Educators and students alike need to consider the implications of algorithmic decisions: who is harmed by potential biases? Which voices are excluded? Teachers may lead students in “algorithm audits,” examining how search results shift based on location or user profile, thus revealing inequities hidden within frictionless interfaces.
  • Responsibility: Social studies aims to develop individuals capable of contributing responsibly to their communities. By fostering responsibility, H.E.A.R.T. insists that learners actively resist the pitfalls of frictionless consumption. Students tasked with analyzing AI-detected ‘facts’ on controversial topics, for instance, carry a responsibility to validate claims, raise questions, and engage in thoughtful dialogue.
  • Thoughtfulness: This final component encourages meta-cognitive reflection, urging students and teachers to ask, “How did we reach this conclusion?” or “What role did AI play in our reasoning process?” By pausing to reflect on how knowledge is constructed, learners guard against the passivity that frictionless platforms can engender.

H.E.A.R.T. thus complements C.O.R.E. by adding explicit ethical and emotional parameters, helping educators shape AI integration in a way that retains humanity and moral purpose in the learning environment.

4.4 Classroom Applications: Structures that Reintroduce Productive Struggle

Implementing C.O.R.E. and H.E.A.R.T. involves intentionally crafting activities that balance the affordances of AI with the tensions, ambiguities, and debates characteristic of authentic social studies inquiry. Below are several exemplars:

  • Algorithmic Bias Audits: Students work in groups to query AI chatbots about historical events involving marginalized communities—e.g., analyzing how the Tulsa Race Massacre or women’s suffrage is narrated. They then cross-reference AI responses with primary sources or credible secondary references, identifying omissions, discrepancies, or biases. C.O.R.E.’s Critical Thinking and Openness converge here, alongside H.E.A.R.T.’s Honesty and Accountability.
  • AI Versus Primary Documents: In a unit on the Civil Rights Movement, learners first review AI-generated summaries of landmark events (e.g., the Montgomery Bus Boycott) before immersing themselves in archival footage, oral histories, or official documents. Class discussions highlight differences between frictionless AI narratives and the messy realities of primary sources, reinforcing Piaget’s idea of disequilibrium as essential for learning.
  • Devil’s Advocate Chatbots: Teachers prompt AI to assume a ‘devil’s advocate’ stance on a contested historical or civic topic. Students, guided by H.E.A.R.T.’s Empathy and Responsibility, must identify the ethical dimensions and potential manipulations within AI’s counterarguments, constructing collaborative rebuttals grounded in evidence.
  • Policy Debates on AI Regulation: In an upper-grade social studies class, students examine emerging AI regulations or educational technology policies, using the C.O.R.E. framework to structure respectful debates and the H.E.A.R.T. framework to ensure that empathy and accountability remain at the forefront. Through these debates, learners grapple with real-world policy complexities, which fosters Dewey’s view of education as preparation for active civic engagement.

4.5 Advantages, Limitations, and Future Directions

While these applications underscore the potential of C.O.R.E. and H.E.A.R.T. to keep friction in AI-mediated classrooms, it is crucial to recognize that frameworks alone cannot resolve all of the challenges posed by frictionless systems. Resource disparities, teacher training gaps, and institutional pressures for test score accountability can all hamper the extent to which educators can implement meaningful, inquiry-based tasks. Additionally, as AI evolves, it may demand ongoing adjustments to these frameworks—particularly as generative models become more sophisticated at mimicking authentic human voice and introducing subtler biases.

Nonetheless, by explicitly weaving together constructivist principles with the ethical guardrails of technoskepticism, these frameworks help close the gap between theory and practice. They make visible the ways in which AI can be harnessed, rather than merely accepted, in the service of deeper cognitive work, ethical awareness, and democratic engagement. Social studies educators, in particular, stand to benefit from adopting these frameworks, given the discipline’s longstanding emphasis on informed civic participation, cultural responsiveness, and moral discernment. We turn next to broader discussions of policy, professional development, and research implications in Section V, charting a path for ensuring that friction serves as a cornerstone rather than a casualty in AI-augmented social studies classrooms.

V. Policy, Professional Development, and Research Directions: Forging a Sustainable Path

In light of the preceding discussions, social studies educators, policymakers, and researchers face a collective imperative: how to guide the integration of AI in ways that preserve the deliberative friction needed for robust learning and civic development. While the C.O.R.E. and H.E.A.R.T. frameworks offer a praxis-based blueprint for classroom-level transformation, these efforts must be supported by broader policy decisions, systematic professional development, and rigorous inquiry into AI’s evolving impacts on K–12 education. This section situates those imperatives within the landscape of social studies, highlighting structural pathways that can sustain ethically grounded, equity-conscious, and inquiry-oriented approaches to AI.

5.1 Policy Considerations: Standards, Equity, and Accountability

5.1.1 Aligning AI Integration with Social Studies Standards

State and national standards in social studies (e.g., NCSS’s C3 Framework, 2013) emphasize inquiry, democratic participation, and cultural responsiveness. Policymakers and educational leaders should therefore revisit technology standards to ensure that generative AI tools do not inadvertently undermine these core disciplinary goals. This includes embedding requirements for student engagement with original, unfiltered sources—reinforcing the friction that fosters historical thinking—and calling for transparent AI usage guidelines in social studies curricula.

For instance, state boards of education can stipulate that AI-supplemented instruction must always be paired with collaborative analysis, multi-sourced evidence, or reflective tasks that encourage deeper inquiry. Such policy directives would mitigate the risk of frictionless AI overshadowing the intellectual rigor essential to civic competence (Kahne & Rogers, 2024).

5.1.2 Addressing Algorithmic Bias and Equitable Access

The problem of algorithmic bias goes beyond mere technological glitches; it implicates fundamental questions about social justice. Relegating certain students—often those from marginalized backgrounds—to oversimplified content or remedial “quick fixes” has grave implications for equity (Buolamwini, 2023; DiResta, 2024). Policymakers and district leaders should mandate equity audits of AI-driven platforms to identify and correct systemic patterns of under-challenging or misrepresenting students based on demographic profiles.

Additionally, bridging the digital divide remains crucial. If frictionless AI adoption proceeds without ensuring equitable device access, high-speed internet availability, and meaningful digital literacy support, it risks entrenching existing disparities rather than dismantling them. Policymakers should thus allocate funding not only for AI tools but also for infrastructure that ensures all learners can engage in complex, inquiry-based tasks.

5.1.3 Accountability and Transparency in AI Deployment

A robust framework of accountability is necessary to guard against ethically and pedagogically detrimental uses of AI in social studies. Much like data privacy regulations, policies must stipulate transparency in AI systems: educators and administrators need to know how algorithms prioritize or filter historical content, which biases may be introduced, and where data is stored or shared.

Public reporting mechanisms—such as requiring vendors to disclose the accuracy and fairness metrics of AI curations—would empower educators and stakeholders to make informed decisions. Coupled with the guidelines encouraging friction-filled activities, these measures foster a system-wide commitment to harnessing AI as a powerful asset rather than an unexamined necessity.

5.2 Professional Development: Building Capacity for Friction

Even the best-designed frameworks and policies falter without sustained, high-quality professional development for teachers. Social studies educators, already balancing complex content and civic objectives, now must learn to integrate AI critically, detect algorithmic bias, and orchestrate friction-laden experiences that maintain integrity of inquiry.

5.2.1 Training in Technoskepticism and Media Literacy

Workshops and in-service sessions can equip teachers with the practical knowledge needed to interrogate AI tools. Drawing on the technoskeptical ethos advanced by Krutka, Heath, and colleagues, PD efforts might include:

  • Algorithmic Literacy: Teachers learn how to conduct “algorithm audits” or guiding students in analyzing how AI responses shift when user parameters are altered.
  • Misinformation Simulations: Educators practice curating lessons where AI deliberately introduces incomplete or biased information, and students must identify and refute it.
  • Pedagogical Reflection: Facilitators prompt teachers to plan or retrofit lessons using the C.O.R.E. and H.E.A.R.T. frameworks, fostering a community of practice where experiences with friction-laden AI engagements can be shared and refined.

5.2.2 Collaborative Inquiry and Mentoring

A system of mentorship—pairing tech-savvy educators with those more versed in inquiry-based social studies—can spark the synergy necessary for meaningful AI integration. Teachers not only share lesson ideas but collectively reflect on how AI might facilitate or jeopardize authentic struggle. This collaboration also encourages teachers to co-develop robust performance tasks, ensuring friction remains a deliberately constructed element within their instructional designs.

5.3 Research Directions: Fostering Evidence-Based Evolution

Finally, the potential for AI to transform social studies education demands rigorous, ongoing research that critically examines both benefits and drawbacks. Rather than merely documenting AI’s ease or novelty, scholars must explore how AI-enabled friction or frictionlessness tangibly affects cognitive outcomes, civic dispositions, and equity.

5.3.1 Empirical Investigations into Classroom Impact

Quantitative and qualitative studies alike can probe the influence of AI-driven tasks on students’ historical reasoning, ethical decision-making, and democratic participation. For instance, quasi-experimental research could compare student performance in classes integrating friction-based AI design against those employing purely frictionless tools, isolating the effects on problem-solving skills, empathic understanding, or retention of knowledge.

5.3.2 Longitudinal Examinations of Equity and Bias

Given the urgency around algorithmic bias and educational tracking, longitudinal research might chart how AI usage over multiple years shapes student agency, critical consciousness, or academic trajectories. Such studies can illuminate whether AI either narrows or widens achievement and engagement gaps among different demographic groups, and how these trajectories relate to broader structural injustices within schooling.

5.3.3 Design-Based Research for Continual Framework Refinement

By employing a design-based research (DBR) methodology, educators and researchers can iteratively refine the C.O.R.E. and H.E.A.R.T. frameworks. Small-scale pilots—where teachers implement friction-laden AI lessons—can generate practical insights into what works and what fails, feeding back into theoretical constructs. This cyclical process not only strengthens the frameworks’ relevance but also fosters a collaborative culture wherein practitioners and scholars co-create more equitable, human-centered AI integration.

5.4 Charting a Sustainable Path

Ultimately, the policy, professional development, and research imperatives outlined here converge on a shared goal: to ensure that friction—manifested as rigorous inquiry, collaborative tension, and moral deliberation—remains integral to AI-enhanced social studies education. Without such strategic scaffolding at systemic levels, local innovations risk being undermined by institutional constraints, inequitable resource distribution, or unchecked commercial pressures from edtech vendors. On the other hand, when robust policies encourage meaningful friction, professional development empowers teachers to navigate AI critically, and empirical research continually refines best practices, generative AI need not eclipse the foundational struggles that drive transformative, democratic learning.

By weaving these components together, educational stakeholders can construct an ecosystem in which AI tools do not simply streamline tasks, but elevate the complexity, depth, and ethical engagement that distinguishes social studies as a cornerstone of democratic education. In doing so, we honor the insights of Dewey, Vygotsky, Piaget, and Freire, ensuring that technology remains harnessed to humanistic and civic ends. Section VI concludes this exploration by synthesizing key findings and reiterating the call for a balanced, reflective, and equity-focused embrace of AI in social studies classrooms.

VI. Conclusion: Reclaiming Productive Struggle in AI-Enhanced Social Studies

The discussions presented throughout this article collectively underscore the critical need to reconcile technological efficiency with deliberate cognitive, ethical, and civic struggles—particularly in the domain of social studies. While generative AI innovations hold great promise for making information more accessible and facilitating tailored learning opportunities, a frictionless educational model threatens to undermine many of the core principles that define meaningful social studies instruction: collaborative inquiry, democratic engagement, historical empathy, and critical thinking.

At the heart of the matter is an enduring paradox in educational reform: efficiency and standardization can risk supplanting the very friction from which intellectual breakthroughs and civic dispositions emerge. Drawing on Dewey’s (1938) notion of experiential democracy, Vygotsky’s (1978) sociocultural emphasis on scaffolded dialogue, Freire’s (1970) liberation-oriented critiques, and Piaget’s (1970) theory of cognitive disequilibrium, we see how true understanding flourishes when learners wrestle with complexity. Such struggle is not a design flaw; rather, it reflects the intricate processes by which individuals learn to contextualize information, navigate multiple perspectives, and internalize moral and civic values.

Social studies education is uniquely positioned to harness this power of constructive friction. By its nature, the discipline compels students to confront contrasting viewpoints, interpret evolving societal challenges, and weigh the tensions between freedom and equality, tradition and change, or nationalism and global cooperation. In short, social studies is not merely about knowing facts; it is about grappling with them, forging interpretations that demand evaluation, deliberation, and ethical consideration. Generative AI tools can either facilitate or disrupt these demanding intellectual tasks, depending largely on how they are integrated and framed.

The C.O.R.E. (Critical Thinking, Openness, Respect, Engagement) and H.E.A.R.T. (Honesty, Empathy, Accountability, Responsibility, Thoughtfulness) frameworks introduced here seek to preserve precisely the elements of friction essential to democratic education. Rather than dismissing AI entirely, these frameworks situate technology as an instrument subject to critical oversight. Educators, guided by C.O.R.E. and H.E.A.R.T., can intentionally design lessons to challenge the reductive summaries or one-click solutions AI platforms often provide. For instance, they can direct students to conduct algorithmic bias audits, evaluate AI-generated historical narratives against primary sources, and stage collaborative debates that keep moral and civic reflexes at the forefront.

Yet, as emphasized in Section V, classroom-level innovation alone does not guarantee success. Without institutional buy-in, supportive professional development structures, and thoughtful policies that promote ethical and contextualized AI usage, educators’ ability to protect friction-laden inquiry will remain constrained. Policymakers can align social studies standards with mandates for original source engagement and equitable AI frameworks. Administrators and teacher-training programs can prioritize technoskepticism and collaborative inquiry, helping teachers not only acquire technical skills but also cultivate the reflective capacities to question how AI shapes the learning process. Meanwhile, research—both longitudinal studies of AI’s impact on equity and design-based projects that refine the C.O.R.E. and H.E.A.R.T. models—can illuminate best practices and help scale them systemically.

Looking forward, social studies educators may face an accelerating pace of AI development. Algorithmic tutoring, generative voice simulations, and immersive simulations are no longer futuristic speculations; they are rapidly becoming mainstream pedagogical options. Whether these tools magnify or diminish democratic education hinges on our collective resolve to retain the friction that fosters reflective and empathetic citizens. If frictionless design is allowed to prevail unchallenged, students risk evolving into passive consumers of automated interpretations, with limited practice in sourcing, corroboration, or ethical accountability. By keeping productive struggle at the center of AI-mediated instruction, we can ensure that emerging technologies remain a tool for critical exploration and a scaffold for informed, democratic participation.
